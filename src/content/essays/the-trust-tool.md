*This essay sits under the* Should we do AI? *and* Are we ready? *questions of the practice's methodology. It explains the 2x2 behind the [Trust Tool](https://trusttool.borck.education/), and how the [Flag System](/writing/the-flag-system) works as a second check inside it.*

## The question nobody asks precisely

A partner looks at an AI output and asks: can we trust this?

The question is reasonable, and it is unanswerable as stated. Trust is not a property of the output alone. It depends on what the output is for, what happens if it is wrong, and how exact it needs to be. A brainstormed list of marketing angles and a cited tax position can come from the same model, in the same minute, and they do not deserve the same scrutiny.

Most firms handle this in one of two ways. They treat every output as risky, so everything gets checked, people tire of checking, and the checks become theatre. Or they treat the tool as reliable because it usually is, and the one output that needed a real check goes out with the firm's name on it.

The Trust Tool is a way out of that binary. It is a 2x2, and it takes about ten seconds to use.

## The two axes

The first axis is consequence: what happens if this is wrong?

At the small end, the output is internal, reversible, and low stakes. A meeting agenda. A first draft of a newsletter. A list of questions for a workshop. If it is wrong, someone notices, edits it, and nothing leaves the building.

At the large end, the output is client-facing, regulated, or hard to reverse. A legal citation. A clinical recommendation. A tax position. A statement in a regulator's submission. A sentence that will be read by a client who relies on it.

The second axis is precision: how exact does this need to be?

At one end, average is fine. The output needs to be useful, readable, and roughly right. A set of possible angles for a campaign. A summary of a public report to decide whether to read the full thing. A first pass at an internal process description.

At the other end, it must be precise. A citation has to exist. A dose has to be correct. A threshold, a rate, a deadline, a section number, a definition. Near enough is not a defence.

Put the two axes together and you get four quadrants.

| | Average is fine | Must be precise |
|---|---|---|
| **Large consequence** | Verify the load-bearing bits | Human-in-the-loop |
| **Small consequence** | Trust with light review | Verify before you ship |

## The four quadrants

### Small consequence, average is fine: trust with light review

This is the green quadrant. The output is low stakes and does not need to be exact. Read it for sense. If it is useful, use it. If it is not, ask again.

Examples:

- A list of possible agenda items for an internal team meeting.
- Three alternative ways to phrase a sentence in a newsletter.
- A summary of a public article to decide whether it is worth reading in full.
- A first draft of an internal process note that a person will rewrite.

The check is light because the cost of being wrong is light. Do not over-engineer this quadrant. Treating it like a red output is how people learn to ignore the process.

### Small consequence, must be precise: verify before you ship

This is a yellow quadrant. The output does not carry much risk on its own, but it contains something exact that has to be right. A citation in an internal memo. A number in a slide. A date in a draft. The consequence is small, but the precision requirement is not.

Examples:

- A citation in an internal research note that a colleague will use later.
- A calculation in a draft slide that will be presented internally.
- A policy reference in a first draft that will be edited by someone else.
- A date or name in a document that will become the source for a later version.

The check is targeted. Find the exact thing and verify it against the primary source. You do not need a second reader for the whole document. You need to check the load-bearing detail.

### Large consequence, average is fine: verify the load-bearing bits

This is the other yellow quadrant. The output is client-facing or high stakes, but the overall content does not need to be exact in every sentence. The narrative, the structure, and the recommendations can be sound while a few facts carry the weight.

Examples:

- A client-facing summary where the overall story is fine but the numbers, dates, and names matter.
- A draft recommendation where the reasoning is sound but the supporting examples must be real.
- A board paper where the argument is yours but the figures have to be checked.
- A proposal where the scope is right but the deliverables and prices must be exact.

The check is selective but serious. Identify the facts that the decision rests on and verify those. Leave the prose alone.

### Large consequence, must be precise: human-in-the-loop

This is the red quadrant. The output is client-facing, regulated, or irreversible, and it must be exact. A legal citation. A clinical recommendation. A tax position. A statement in a regulator's submission. A recommendation that crosses into advice.

The check is independent review and sign-off. Not a spot check. Not a second model. A person with the authority and the expertise to own the output. The AI can draft, summarise, and challenge. It cannot carry the professional act.

## Why the quadrants are not a hierarchy

It is tempting to read the grid as a ladder from green to red, with the two yellow quadrants as stops on the way. They are not. The two yellow quadrants require different checks.

In the small-consequence, must-be-precise quadrant, you are checking a detail. The output can be internal and low stakes, but the citation still has to exist. In the large-consequence, average-is-fine quadrant, you are checking the load-bearing facts in something that will leave the building. The narrative can be yours; the numbers cannot be invented.

That difference matters. A firm that treats both as "yellow" and applies the same check will either over-check the internal memo or under-check the client paper. The quadrant tells you what kind of check to run.

## The two failure modes

The first failure mode is uniform alarm. Everything is treated as red. Every output gets a second reader. Every draft becomes a review meeting. People learn that the process is a tax, and they route around it. The output that actually needed the check gets the same treatment as the one that did not, which means it gets no real treatment at all.

The second failure mode is uniform trust. The tool is usually right, so the firm stops classifying. The green outputs are fine, the yellow outputs are fine, and then a red output arrives looking exactly like the others. Fluent, complete, confident. It goes out.

The Trust Tool is designed to make the middle visible. Most professional work lives in the two yellow quadrants. That is where the check has to be specific, and where a firm gets the most value from a shared vocabulary.

## How to use it in a team

The tool is more useful as a conversation than as a poster.

Take ten real outputs from the firm's work. Sort them into the four quadrants. Do it individually first, then compare. The disagreements are the useful part. One person puts a client summary in green because it is only a summary. Another puts it in red because it goes to a client. The argument about the quadrant is the argument about the firm's standard.

Then agree a check for each quadrant:

- Green: read for sense. No second reader.
- Small consequence, must be precise: verify the exact detail against the primary source.
- Large consequence, average is fine: identify the load-bearing facts and verify those.
- Red: independent review and sign-off by someone with the authority to own it.

Write the checks down. Put them in the AI use policy. Then use the same ten scenarios next quarter and see whether the room still disagrees.

## The flags inside the quadrants

The Trust Tool tells you which quadrant an output belongs in. It does not tell you what to look for once you are there.

That is the job of the [Flag System](/writing/the-flag-system). It is a second check, not a separate scale:

- Green flags: low scrutiny is appropriate. Read for sense.
- Yellow flags: missing provenance. A cited source you have not opened. A number you have not seen elsewhere. A summary of a document you have not spot-checked. A rule applied to facts you have not tested.
- Red flags: a citation that fits too neatly, advice that crosses into a professional act, a confident claim on a contested or jurisdiction-specific question, an irreversible action.

The flags are not a traffic light for whether to proceed with a project. They are a reading discipline for the output in front of you. Project-level go, pivot, and kill decisions belong to a different layer: the lifecycle gates that sit between foundation, pilot, and scale.

Keeping those layers separate matters. The Trust Tool and the flags are about the output. The lifecycle gates are about the initiative. A firm can have a green output inside a project that should be killed, and a red output inside a project that is worth scaling.

## What the tool does not do

It does not verify the output for you. It allocates scrutiny. The checking is still the firm's work.

It does not replace professional judgment, the firm's policy, or the regulator's expectations. It gives those things a shared vocabulary.

It does not make a bad process good. If the firm has no review culture, a 2x2 will not create one. It will only make the absence visible.

It is not a compliance certificate. It is a habit.

## A ten-second habit

Before you use an output, ask two questions:

1. What happens if this is wrong?
2. How exact does it need to be?

The answers put the output in a quadrant. The quadrant tells you what kind of check to run. That is the whole tool.

If you want to build the instinct, play the [Trust Tool](https://trusttool.borck.education/). It gives you ten scenarios, a domain pack, and a score. The score is not the point. The disagreements are.
