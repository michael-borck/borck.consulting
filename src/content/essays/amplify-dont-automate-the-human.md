*This essay sits primarily under the* Should we do AI? *and* How do we do AI? *questions of the practice's [methodology](/method): the difference between amplifying a person's work and automating the person out of it.*

## The first automation

The first automation a small firm usually reaches for is the client update. It is repetitive. It takes time. It is easy to draft. That is exactly why it is dangerous.

A partner asks a model to turn the week's notes into a client update. The draft is fluent. It is accurate enough. It is sent. The client reads it, replies politely, and something small goes missing. The update arrived on time. The attention did not.

The saving is real. The loss is quiet. That is the problem with over-automating a regulated practice. The cost lands in the relationship, months later, when the client is deciding whether to stay.

I wrote about this pattern in *The Human Edge*, in the chapter on designing the human in. The same chapter has the line this essay builds on: AI is a multiplier, and a multiplier does not choose what it multiplies.

## A multiplier does not choose

Feed a multiplier a clear brief, a checked draft, and a decision that stays with a person, and it multiplies capacity. Feed it a relationship and remove the person, and it multiplies the absence.

Used well, AI raises the level of the work. There is more room for framing, checking, deciding and connecting. More people can take part, because the cost of a first pass falls. A junior can attempt work that used to be reserved for someone with ten years of context. A partner can spend the saved hour on the client rather than the file.

Used badly, it automates the human moment that made the work valuable. A client update, a complaint reply, a performance conversation. The words can be drafted. The presence is the service.

The distinction is not about the tool. The same model can do both, and the workflow decides which one you get.

## The efficiency case is real

AI can draft updates, summarise correspondence, prepare meeting notes, and turn a file into a first pass. For a five-partner firm, that can save hours a week. It can reduce the Sunday-night admin and make a junior more useful sooner.

The efficiency case is real. It is also incomplete.

The useful question is what this interaction is for. Whether AI can draft it is the easy part. A client update carries information, but it also signals that someone is paying attention. A bad-news call is the moment the client learns whether you will stand with them. A pricing conversation tests trust as much as it states a number.

When you automate the interaction, you may keep the information and lose the signal.

## What the client is buying

In a regulated firm, the document is the artefact. The client is paying for the judgement that says the document is right, the attention that notices what changed, and the accountability of a person whose name is on the file.

That is why the relationship is the product. The work can be standardised. The relationship cannot. The client is buying a person who knows the matter, remembers the context, and will tell them the truth when the answer is inconvenient.

AI can help that person. It can prepare the file, draft the options, and surface the questions. It cannot be that person.

## The connection layer

Every regulated practice has a connection layer. It is the set of interactions where the relationship is either strengthened or eroded:

| Touchpoint | What AI can do | What must stay human |
|---|---|---|
| Bad news and apologies | Draft the facts and the options | The call, the tone, and the accountability |
| Advice and recommendations | Prepare the analysis and the alternatives | The judgement and the sign-off |
| Conflict and complaints | Summarise the history and the correspondence | The conversation and the remedy |
| Onboarding | Prepare the pack and the checklist | The welcome and the first impression |
| Pricing and scope | Draft the letter and the schedule | The conversation about value and risk |
| Routine updates | Draft the update and flag the changes | The review, the judgement, and the relationship |

The pattern is consistent. Automate the preparation. Keep the relationship.

The point is what the automation is for. A firm that automates the preparation gives the partner more time for the connection. A firm that automates the connection gives the client less reason to stay.

## Ask what is lost

Before automating a step, ask what is lost if no person does it. That is a better question than whether AI can do it, because the model will usually say yes.

Two things go missing when a human step disappears.

The first is the signal. A person who reads the file before sending the update notices that the client has gone quiet, or that a date moved, or that the tone is wrong for the week the client has had. The model drafts from the notes. It does not notice what is absent from them.

The second is the practice. The person who used to write the update learns how the client reacts, how a matter is assembled, and where a judgement is tested. Remove the step and the learning goes with it.

Presence is the service. The words are the artefact. The fact that a person with context read them and decided they were right is what the client is paying for.

## The team layer

There is a second relationship at risk: the one inside the firm.

Junior staff learn by doing the work. If AI does the first pass and nobody reviews it with them, they learn to accept output rather than to produce it. The firm saves time now and loses the next generation of judgement later.

This is the apprenticeship problem. It is easy to miss because the output looks fine. The work is done. The learning is not.

*Conversation, Not Delegation* calls the other version of this the delegation trap. You accept a result you cannot explain, and the understanding never arrives. *The Human Edge* puts the team version plainly: if AI takes the easy work, the reps disappear, and somebody has to put the practice back on purpose.

The fix is to make the learning visible. Keep one step human on purpose. Pick the step that teaches the work, the one where a junior learns what good looks like, or where the partner keeps a feel for the material. Use AI as a multiplayer tool: a shared brief, a shared check, a shared conversation about what changed.

A solo prompt box is not a firm capability. A team that can brief, check, and challenge together is.

## The chair test

The practical test is simpler than a framework. Does the workflow add a chair at the table, or remove the person already sitting there?

Adding a chair looks like a junior who can now attempt the first pass with a partner's brief, a specialist pulled into a conversation that used to happen without them, or a client who gets a faster answer because the preparation no longer consumes the week.

Removing a chair looks like a fluent update with nobody behind it, a complaint reply that answers the facts and misses the apology, or a performance conversation conducted by a template.

Before you automate an interaction, ask two questions:

1. If this interaction were automated, what would the client lose?
2. Who on the team needs to learn from this task?

If the answer to either is "something that matters", keep a human in the loop. The relationship or the learning is the point, even where the model could do the job.

This is the same logic as the Trust Tool. Consequence is relational as well as legal or financial. A client update can be a green task on precision and a red task on relationship. The tool does not capture that unless you make relationship consequence part of the assessment.

Make it explicit. Add a column to the workflow: "relationship consequence." Mark the touchpoints where the client needs a person. Then decide what AI prepares and what a person delivers.

## What to do

Start with the work, not the tool.

1. Map the workflow. Use the Opportunity Finder to spot, assign, and prioritise.
2. Mark the connection layer. Which touchpoints carry the relationship?
3. Keep those human. Let AI prepare the file, the options, and the draft.
4. Keep one step human on purpose for the apprenticeship.
5. Name the human moment. Write down what a person adds that the model cannot: attention, context, accountability, presence.
6. Set the gate. Use the First Move Canvas to decide what you will scale, pivot, or kill at 30 days.

The goal is to automate the right things. The preparation is usually safe. The relationship usually is not.

A firm that gets this right looks more human without looking less efficient. The client gets the speed of AI and the attention of a person. That is a better product than either one alone.
