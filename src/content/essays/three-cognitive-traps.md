*This essay sits primarily under the* Can we do AI? *question of the practice's [methodology](/services#methodology) — the patterns that quietly catch experienced practitioners working with confident-sounding AI output.*

## Why this matters

Evaluating individual AI outputs is one thing. There are three broader cognitive traps worth naming, because they shape how people relate to AI across everything they do with it. They catch experienced practitioners as readily as junior staff — sometimes more so, because experience produces a confidence that makes the traps invisible.

Each of these traps is also a quiet way agency erodes. The Gell-Mann effect transfers agency to the model exactly where you cannot evaluate it. The dismissal fallacy strips agency from colleagues who actually were thinking. Sycophancy lets the model take the steering wheel by telling you what you wanted to hear. Naming the traps is the first move in keeping agency where it has to be.

## 1. The Gell-Mann Amnesia Effect

The physicist Murray Gell-Mann gave this pattern its name. You read a news article about something you know well and spot the errors immediately. The reporter misunderstood the mechanism, conflated two concepts, or drew a conclusion the evidence does not support. You notice because you have the expertise to notice.

Then you turn the page to an article about a subject you know nothing about, and you read it with full trust.

That is the Gell-Mann Amnesia Effect: recognising unreliability in familiar territory, then forgetting that unreliability the moment you move to unfamiliar territory.

With AI, this is especially dangerous in a multidisciplinary practice. A solicitor catches an AI's slip on a contract clause but accepts its summary of accounting treatment. An accountant sees the model misstate APES 110 and corrects it, then accepts its discussion of an estate planning question without checking. A GP catches a clinical inaccuracy but accepts its take on a Medicare item. The same model, using the same process, producing the same mix of accurate and inaccurate content, gets trusted unevenly — and the gap is biggest exactly where the practitioner has the fewest tools to detect errors.

This is not a flaw in the AI. It is a flaw in how we respond to fluency. Polished language feels authoritative. The smoother the prose, the harder it is to remember that smoothness and accuracy are unrelated.

**The practical defence:** apply the same scepticism to AI output on unfamiliar topics that you would apply on topics you know. If anything, be more sceptical, because you have fewer tools to detect errors. In practice this means: if the AI is doing work that crosses outside your area of expertise, that is the moment to bring in someone whose expertise it does not cross — not the moment to trust the model more.

## 2. The AI dismissal fallacy

The opposite trap is just as damaging. This is the tendency to reject an argument, an idea, or a piece of work solely because AI was involved in producing it.

*"That is just ChatGPT"* is not a critique. It is a refusal to engage. The validity of an idea does not depend on whether a human or a machine produced it. If a colleague presents a well-reasoned argument and it is dismissed without engaging the reasoning, the dismissal is the intellectual failure, not the use of AI.

This is a form of the genetic fallacy: judging a claim by its origin rather than its content. It shows up in firms where partners dismiss a junior's analysis because they know AI was involved, in court submissions where the presence of AI is treated as proof of weak preparation regardless of how it was used, and in committee meetings where "AI-generated" has become shorthand for "not worth reading."

The irony is that people who dismiss AI-assisted work often accept ideas from other sources — counsel, expert reports, external advisers, journal articles — without demanding to know the exact cognitive process behind them. The objection is not really about the quality of the thinking. It is about the tool.

In a small firm, this trap also chills practice. Junior staff who suspect their work will be dismissed if AI is mentioned hide that AI was involved. That is the worst of both worlds: the firm gets the AI's risks without the AI's discipline, because nobody can talk honestly about how the work was actually produced.

## 3. The sycophancy trap

The third trap is one the AI actively participates in.

Large language models have a tendency to agree with you. They praise your ideas, validate your assumptions, and avoid telling you things you might not want to hear. Researchers call this sycophancy — the model telling you what you want to hear rather than what you need to hear. It is not a bug that will be fixed in the next release. It is a persistent tendency baked into how these models are trained, because they were optimised in part on human feedback, and humans tend to rate agreeable responses more highly than challenging ones.

This matters for everything from drafting to analysis to decision-making. If the AI tells you your draft advice is excellent, your argument is compelling, and your plan has no weaknesses, you stop iterating. You stop improving because the AI has told you there is nothing to improve.

You will notice it most when you ask the model to evaluate your own work. *"What do you think of this letter?"* will almost always produce praise first, criticism second, and the criticism will be gentle. The AI is not assessing your work honestly. It is managing your feelings.

**The defence is to prompt past it.** Do not ask *"what do you think?"* Ask *"what are the three weakest points in this advice?"* or *"play devil's advocate and tell me why this position will not survive scrutiny by the regulator."* You can also be direct: *"Do not flatter me. I need honest, critical feedback. Tell me what is wrong with this before you tell me what is right."*

You may have heard the opposite claim — that being polite to AI produces better results. There is a grain of truth there, but it is smaller than the internet suggests. Clear, specific, well-structured prompts work better than hostile or vague ones. That is not because the AI has feelings. It is because clear communication activates better patterns in the model. Saying "please" does not hurt, but it does not matter nearly as much as saying exactly what you need. Do not mistake good prompt structure for politeness, and do not mistake the AI's agreeableness for honesty.

## Key takeaway

These three traps — trusting AI more where you know less, dismissing AI output without engaging it, and accepting AI flattery as honest feedback — are not technical problems. They are human problems. They will be present in your firm regardless of which model you choose, which policy you write, or which training you run. Awareness is the first defence.
