## 1. Why critique skills matter

Using AI in a regulated practice is like hiring a brilliant but inexperienced consultant: fast, confident, and sometimes dangerously wrong.

Without critique skills, your firm risks:

- **Over-engineered recommendations** that no one has the time, budget, or appetite to implement
- **Missing legal, professional, or risk issues** that create real exposure for the firm and the client
- **Suggestions that sound good but do not fit your practice, your jurisdiction, or your client's situation**
- **One-size-fits-all advice** that ignores the constraints, history, and culture that make a small firm work

The pattern is the same across law, medicine, accounting, and financial advice: uncritical AI acceptance leads to poor decisions, complaints, regulator attention, insurer letters, and wasted partner hours unpicking work that should have been right the first time.

Your professional edge is not "using AI." It is **reviewing, questioning, and improving** what AI gives you. The five-step framework below is what agency looks like in practice — the active work of choosing what to accept, refine, or reject before anything goes out the door.

## 2. The five-step critique framework

Use this with any AI-generated recommendation, draft, plan, or analysis before it leaves the firm.

### Step 1 — Comprehension check: do I actually understand this?

**Red flags:** vague jargon, unclear rationale, no clear problem statement, conclusions that float free of premises.

**Ask:**

- "Explain this in plain English as if for the client's spouse."
- "What specific question does this answer, and how?"
- "Break this into the three or four substantive moves you are making."

### Step 2 — Simplicity check: is this practical for this matter?

**Red flags:** the recommendation requires extra people, expensive tools, or new processes the firm does not have.

**Ask:**

- "What is the minimum viable version of this?"
- "Rewrite this for a small firm with limited time and a defined fee."
- "Show a phased version over the next ninety days, not a five-year plan."

### Step 3 — Legal, professional, and risk check: what are the implications?

**Red flags:** no mention of compliance, privacy, professional obligations, jurisdiction, or risk; advice that crosses from drafting into practice.

**Ask:**

- "What legal, regulatory, or professional issues could this raise in an Australian context?"
- "What are the main risks to the client, and to the firm?"
- "Who could be harmed or disadvantaged by this recommendation?"
- "Are the citations or section references correct? Quote the actual text and source."

### Step 4 — Context check: what is AI assuming about this client and matter?

**Red flags:** assumes a US legal framework, a different tax year, a clinical context that does not match, a client who has resources or sophistication they do not have.

**Ask:**

- "List the assumptions you are making about jurisdiction, the client's situation, and the available facts."
- "How would this need to change for [specific real constraint]?"
- "Give an alternative that works with the actual facts I provided."

### Step 5 — Stakeholder check: how will different parties experience this?

**Red flags:** only the loudest voice in the room is considered; no sense of how the regulator, the counterparty, the family, the firm, or the staff might respond.

**Ask:**

- "Walk through this from the perspective of [other party / regulator / client's family / opposing counsel]."
- "What pushback should I expect, and from whom?"
- "What communication and support would be needed for this to land properly?"

How the steps map to broader critique principles:

- Comprehension → **Purpose**
- Simplicity, Context → **Plausibility**
- Legal/Professional/Risk, Context → **Evidence & Stakes**
- Stakeholder → **Stakes & Action**

## 3. Common AI problems and better directions

When you apply the framework, you will see the same patterns in AI output again and again.

**Over-engineered solutions**

- *AI:* ten-step processes, multiple new systems, daily activities for a small firm with two partners and three staff.
- *Better direction:* "Start with the 80/20. What twenty per cent of this would deliver most of the value for our actual practice?"

**Ignoring compliance, professional obligations, or jurisdiction**

- *AI:* recommendations framed in US terms, "just migrate everything to the cloud," advice that crosses into a regulated act.
- *Better direction:* "Rewrite this for the Australian context, respecting [APP / LPUL / AHPRA / APES 110 / RG 175 — pick what applies], and be explicit where the answer differs from US guidance you may have been trained on."

**One-size-fits-all**

- *AI:* the same advice for every client regardless of size, sector, or sophistication.
- *Better direction:* "Show how this would differ for two different client types I see — for example, [a sole trader] versus [an SME with multiple entities]."

Treat these as warning labels: when you see them, slow down and apply the full five-step critique.

## 4. Reusable prompts

Lift or adapt these when working with AI on firm work.

**Requesting a simpler, workable version**

> "This solution looks more complex than the firm can realistically deliver inside a fixed fee. We are a [size/type] practice with limited time and standard tools. Give me a simpler version that focuses on essentials and uses what we already have."

**Checking legal, professional, and risk issues**

> "Before I act on this, list the key legal, compliance, privacy, and professional risks the recommendation could create in an Australian [legal / medical / accounting / financial advisory] context. Suggest two or three safeguards for the most serious ones, and flag any citations or section references that should be verified against primary source."

**Testing organisational and matter fit**

> "The client has [specific real constraints]. Rewrite this recommendation so it still works under those conditions, or explain why it might fail."

**Anticipating resistance**

> "What pushback should I expect from [the other side / the client's family / the regulator / a co-trustee], and how would I address each in a single paragraph?"

## 5. Documenting your critique

A simple file note pattern reinforces good habits and supports any later complaint, audit, or insurer query.

```text
AI interaction note – Matter [reference]
Date: [date]
Tool used: [e.g. M365 Copilot / vendor X / consumer ChatGPT with mock data]
Question I asked: [the actual question]
AI's first response: [1–2 sentences summary]
My critique: [what I challenged, changed, or rejected]
Verified against primary source: [yes/no — and what I checked]
Final version I am acting on: [1–2 sentences]
Outstanding items I still need to check: [data, professional advice, partner sign-off]
Lesson for next time: [short reflection]
```

This is not bureaucratic overhead. In a regulated context it is the file note you would want to exist if a complaint, audit, or insurer query landed in twelve months.

## 6. Red-flag checklist

Before acting on any AI-generated recommendation, scan for these questions:

- ☐ Can I explain this clearly to the client and to a partner in a few minutes?
- ☐ Does it comply with relevant Australian law, professional standards, and the firm's risk appetite?
- ☐ Do we have the time, skills, data, and tools to deliver it inside the fee?
- ☐ How will the affected parties experience it — is it fair, transparent, and defensible?
- ☐ What are the main risks if this goes wrong, and how big are they?
- ☐ Have I checked how different stakeholders (client, regulator, counterparty) would react?
- ☐ Is there a simpler version that delivers most of the value with less exposure?

Use this as a sense-check after the five-step framework.

## 7. A quick practice exercise

Try this once with a real (or realistic) matter:

1. **Choose a real-world challenge** the firm regularly faces — drafting a particular kind of letter, structuring a particular kind of transaction, explaining a particular condition or position to a client.
2. **Ask an AI tool for a recommendation or draft.**
3. **Apply the five-step critique** and write down at least one red flag at each step.
4. **Ask follow-up questions** using the prompts above until you have a version you might realistically use.
5. **Document the interaction** with the short template.

This is how professional judgement about AI is built in practice: not by accepting the first output, but by interrogating it until it is genuinely useful — and by making the interrogation visible.
