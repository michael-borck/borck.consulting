## Purpose

This is a practical companion for principals and partners thinking about how AI fits inside a regulated practice. Use it to:

- Frame AI as a **professional ethics and integrity opportunity**, not just a misconduct risk
- Talk with staff about **responsible AI use and data governance** in a way that keeps the conversation honest
- Design workflows that are **AI-aware and integrity-resilient** without becoming unworkable

---

## 1. Reframing AI and integrity

The question I hear most from partners:

> "Aren't we just teaching staff to cheat with AI?"

The more useful professional question is:

> "How do we develop our team so they use AI responsibly in client work?"

Why the reframing matters:

- **Old framing:** AI is a threat. The goal is to prevent or detect its use.
- **New framing:** AI literacy is a professional capability. The goal is to develop transparent, critical, responsible use.

Your team will use AI tools. They are using them now. The real risk is not that they use AI. It is that they use it **incompetently or covertly**. A blanket ban does not stop AI use; it stops AI use the firm can see, supervise, and stand behind. That is the worst available outcome.

## 2. A three-part framework for ethical AI use

A simple structure that travels well across legal, medical, accounting, and advisory practice.

### 2.1 Transparency, not prohibition

**Principle:** make AI use explicit and supervisable rather than hidden and policed.

**In practice:**

- State clearly **when AI is expected, permitted, or not permitted** in the firm
- Provide **approved tools and recommended patterns** rather than leaving staff to choose
- Supervise **how staff use and critique AI**, not just whether they use it
- Reward staff who **identify and correct AI's errors**, particularly on regulated content

**Why it strengthens integrity:** when AI use is transparent, staff talk openly about their tools and reasoning. When it is simply banned, the use moves to phones the firm cannot see, and that is where complaints, breaches, and uninsured outcomes come from.

### 2.2 Critical oversight, not blind reliance

**Principle:** AI is a tool that requires human judgement, not an authority to trust.

**In practice:**

- Set up workflows where staff must **critique, correct, or override** AI output before it is acted on
- Ask staff to show **what AI got wrong or missed** — particularly citations, jurisdictions, doses, rates, deadlines
- Supervise the **quality of the critique and the improvement**, not the AI's first answer
- Build a small library of **real examples** from your own practice: hallucination, bias, oversimplification, wrong jurisdiction, fabricated citation

**Why it strengthens integrity:** thoughtful use is harder than avoidance. Staff practise the professional habits — checking context, evidence, and consequences — that they need anyway, and your file note discipline becomes an asset rather than a forensic exercise.

### 2.3 Professional relevance, not abstract policy

**Principle:** connect AI policy to how AI shows up in the actual work, in your sector.

**In practice:**

- Frame guidance in terms of **real scenarios**: drafting a client letter, summarising a discovery bundle, paraphrasing a clinical guideline, exploring a tax position
- Discuss **professional accountability** explicitly: who is responsible when AI is wrong? (You are. Always.) Reference your professional obligations — APP, LPUL, AHPRA, APES 110, ASIC RG 175 — directly.
- Make **AI literacy and responsible use visible in the firm's competency expectations**, not just in a policy document filed somewhere

**Why it strengthens integrity:** staff see AI not as a shortcut around their professional obligations, but as a tool they must learn to handle within them.

The thread running through all three principles is *agency* — the practitioner's ability to steer the work and stand behind it. Transparency makes agency visible. Critical oversight is agency in action. Professional relevance ties agency to the obligations that already presume it. A firm's AI policy is, in effect, a policy on whose agency is allowed to be where.

## 3. Data governance in practice

Most small firms in Australia run Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace. Both offer enterprise AI options that operate inside your tenant under your existing data agreements. Practice management, accounting, and clinical software vendors are increasingly building AI features under their existing contracts. Staff also have access to consumer tools (free ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, etc.) on their personal devices — you cannot fully control which tools they touch, but you can give them the framework to make informed choices.

### Enterprise vs consumer tools — the simple view

**Enterprise / approved tools** (e.g. Microsoft 365 Copilot, Gemini for Workspace, vendor-embedded AI in your practice systems):

- Data is processed inside your tenant under your existing agreements
- Not used to train public models
- Covered by your existing privacy and information security posture
- Appropriate for **internal documents, matter content, and (with care) client-identifiable information** within the relevant policy

**Consumer / free tools** (e.g. ChatGPT free, public Gemini, public Claude):

- Conversations may be retained and may be used to improve services
- Less transparent data handling
- No firm-level contract or guarantees
- Appropriate for **non-sensitive work, generic content, learning, and mock-data exploration** — never for client-identifiable information

### Design strategies that encourage better data handling

Rather than trying to ban tools you cannot see, set up patterns that naturally pull staff toward safer practice:

- Use **fictional or generic scenarios** for training and prompt experimentation
- Require **de-identification** before any real data is used in a non-enterprise tool
- Ask for **process documentation** (key prompts, decisions, what was changed and why) on regulated work
- Be explicit about **which tools are recommended** and *why* — tooling without rationale gets ignored

## 4. A clear AI use statement, staff-facing

Embed a short AI section in your firm handbook, induction pack, or team meeting.

### Example structure to adapt

**AI use in this firm**

- **AI is expected when:**
  - Completing specific activities where AI is part of the workflow (e.g. summarising a long document for an internal note, generating a first pass on a generic letter)
- **AI is permitted when:**
  - Brainstorming, generating discussion options, checking grammar or clarity, exploring concepts you do not fully understand yet
- **AI is not permitted when:**
  - The work is sensitive enough that the data should not leave the firm's approved environment
  - The matter involves [sector-specific carve-outs, e.g. court-bound documents, clinical decisions, regulated advice], unless explicitly approved
  - You cannot defend the work in your own words at the end

**When you use AI in firm work, you must:**

- Treat it as a **support for your thinking**, not a replacement
- **Critically evaluate** outputs and adapt them for the matter, the client, and the law
- Be able to **explain and defend** your work in your own words to the supervising partner
- Note AI use in the matter file where it materially affected the work

Using AI to avoid thinking, or signing off AI output that you cannot defend, is a professional integrity issue regardless of whether the policy mentions it explicitly. Your registration and your insurer both already know that.

## 5. Designing integrity-aware workflows

Small workflow changes make work much more resistant to inappropriate AI use while still allowing transparent, critical use.

### 5.1 Focus on process, not just product

- *Weak supervision:* "Send me the letter when it's ready."
- *Stronger:* "Send me the letter, plus a one-paragraph note on what AI did, what you changed, and one thing you would have done differently."
- → The partner reviews **visible process**, not just polished output.

### 5.2 Make judgement visible

- *Weak supervision:* "Recommend a position."
- *Stronger:* "Here are two AI-generated angles on this matter. Critique each one, choose the better, and explain what AI has missed or misunderstood given the facts."
- → AI becomes the **starting point**, not the answer.

### 5.3 Use real context

- *Vulnerable:* generic questions any model can answer generically.
- *Stronger:* tie work to the **specific client, matter, and history** that staff actually know.
- → Generic AI output will not fit well without substantial professional adaptation, and the partner can hear the difference.

### 5.4 Supervise the conversation, not just the product

For training and capability building, ask juniors occasionally to submit **the AI conversation transcript** along with their work, with short annotations on the key moments where they steered or rejected the output. This is what makes "conversation, not delegation" visible. AI can produce convincing reflections, but a genuine iterative conversation is harder to fake.

## 6. Quick red-flag checklist

Use these as prompts for supportive follow-up, not automatic accusations.

- A sudden, unexplained jump in quality compared with earlier work
- Output that **ignores key contextual details** the matter carries (wrong jurisdiction, wrong client structure, dated guidance)
- No real evidence of a **genuine conversation** with the model on work that should have required one
- Staff member **cannot explain or defend** the submitted work in plain language under partner questioning

Useful follow-up moves:

- Ask them to **walk you through their thinking** on a specific part
- Offer a short **oral or written clarification** opportunity
- Use the moment as a **teaching point** about checking output and staying accountable — the goal is capability, not punishment

---

## 7. The action step

Draft or refine a short **AI use statement** for the firm. Use the structure above:

1. When AI use is **expected**
2. When AI use is **permitted**
3. When AI use is **not permitted**
4. What staff **must do** when they use AI (critical engagement, explanation, data governance)
5. How this connects to **professional integrity and your specific obligations** rather than to rule-compliance for its own sake

Aim for a tone that is **clear, direct, and constructive**. The goal is not to scare staff away from AI. It is to help them use it transparently, critically, and responsibly — which is also what your professional standards body and your insurer are quietly hoping you are doing.
